# GTBank Customer Churn Analysis

## 📊 Project Overview

This project analyses customer churn patterns using a dataset of 10,000 GTBank customer records. The objective was to identify the key characteristics associated with customers leaving the bank and provide data-driven recommendations that could support customer retention.

The analysis found that 2,037 customers had churned, representing an overall churn rate of 20.37%.

## 🎯 Project Objectives

- Measure the overall customer churn rate
- Identify customer groups with the highest churn
- Examine the relationship between churn and customer activity
- Analyse churn across age, gender, location and account balance
- Investigate the impact of product ownership and customer tenure
- Identify practical opportunities for improving customer retention

## 🧹 Data Preparation

The dataset was prepared and validated before analysis. This included:

- Checking Customer ID and row counts across 10,000 records
- Removing personal surnames from the analysis view
- Converting the Exited field into retained and churned outcomes
- Grouping age, tenure, balance and product ownership
- Standardising category labels
- Checking numeric fields for valid ranges
- Validating the final totals of 7,963 retained and 2,037 churned customers

## 📈 Key Findings

- Overall customer churn was *20.37%, with **2,037 customers* leaving the bank.
- *Middle-aged customers* recorded a churn rate of *49.45%*, making them a major high-risk group.
- *Inactive customers* had a churn rate of *26.85%, compared with **14.27%* among active customers.
- Customers with *two products* had the strongest retention, with only *7.58% churn*.
- Customers with *three products* recorded *82.71% churn, while the small group with four products recorded **100% churn*.
- Female customers recorded *25.07% churn, compared with **16.46% for male customers*.
- Port Harcourt had the highest location-based churn rate at *22.00%*, although differences between locations were relatively small.
- Customer tenure and credit-card ownership showed comparatively weak relationships with churn.

## 💡 Business Recommendations

1. *Create inactivity alerts* to identify customers whose transaction or digital activity begins to decline.

2. *Review product-heavy accounts*, particularly customers with three or four products, to identify possible issues involving product suitability, fees or service experience.

3. *Develop life-stage retention strategies* for middle-aged and senior customers.

4. *Investigate the gender churn gap* by comparing complaints, product usage, service outcomes and channel preferences.

5. *Strengthen customer onboarding* during the first two years of the customer relationship.

6. *Protect high-value customers* through proactive reviews of customers holding significant account balances.

7. *Develop a churn prediction model* using factors such as age, activity, product ownership, balance and gender.

8. *Track retention outcomes*, including customers contacted, complaints resolved, retained balances and churn avoided.

## 🔑 Conclusion

The analysis shows that customer churn is not evenly distributed across the customer base. Customer activity, age and product ownership provide some of the strongest indicators of retention risk.

The findings suggest that early identification of disengaged customers, targeted engagement with high-risk groups and proactive review of product-heavy accounts could help strengthen customer retention.
